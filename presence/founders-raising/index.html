What should a founder wear to pitch investors in Europe?

Wear what makes your clothes the least interesting thing in the room. In most European venture meetings that is a dark fine-gauge knit or unstructured jacket, plain trousers and clean leather shoes. No tie, no logo, no new suit.

The rest of this page is the detail that makes that sentence usable: how the formality floor moves between European cities, what to actually buy and what to alter, and the specific faults that cost you in a partner meeting. It is written to be acted on without hiring anyone.

Why the default is understatement

European venture has a narrow formality band and it sits lower than most first-time founders expect. The reason is not that investors are casual people. It is that the industry codes visible effort in dress as a signal about where your attention is.

A founder in an obviously new, sharply pressed suit is read — unfairly, quickly, and without anyone saying it out loud — as someone who prepared for the meeting rather than someone who came from work. The suit is doing the opposite of what it was bought to do. Meanwhile, the founder in a good dark knit reads as though they were pulled out of a working day, which is the impression the whole room is implicitly rewarding.

This is a convention, not a law of nature, and it is local. It holds across the venture rooms of London, Berlin, Amsterdam, Stockholm, Copenhagen and Helsinki. It holds much less in Milan, Paris and Zurich. It reverses outright in a room where your buyer is a bank.

The read behind this: a decade working as an international model across eight markets — Cape Town, Milan, Shanghai, Hong Kong, Seoul, Bali, Mumbai and Istanbul — which is a decade of being assessed on precisely this calibration, in about three seconds, thousands of times.

The formality floor, market by market

Calibrate to the city you are pitching in, not the city you live in. Roughly, from most dressed-down to most dressed-up:

European venture formality, approximate
MarketDefault that reads correctly
BerlinThe floor. Knit or overshirt, dark denim acceptable in most seed rooms. A jacket is not expected and can read as distance.
Stockholm, Copenhagen, HelsinkiVery dressed down but very precise. Quality and condition are read closely; formality is not. Fine knitwear, clean minimal shoes.
AmsterdamSimilar to the Nordics, marginally more relaxed about condition.
LondonThe broadest band. Knit-plus-jacket is safe everywhere from seed to growth. Fintech and anything touching the City runs noticeably more formal.
Munich, ZurichMeaningfully more formal. A jacket is close to expected; a well-chosen suit is not a mistake here.
Paris, MilanThe highest floor. Under-dressing is read as carelessness rather than as focus. Tailoring is understood and assessed properly.

Two adjustments on top of the table. Stage runs one notch above meetings in the same city — a demo day in Berlin is still dressed up relative to a Berlin partner meeting. And later stages run one notch above earlier ones everywhere, because the people in the room are increasingly institutional.

The actual kit

The knit

A fine-gauge merino crew neck in charcoal, navy or black does more work than any other single garment. Fine gauge, not chunky — it should sit close without clinging, and it should not look like a winter sweater. Expect €80–150. Buy two, in different colours, and retire them the moment they pill at the forearm, which is at about eighteen months of real use.

The jacket

Unstructured or lightly structured, meaning minimal shoulder padding and a soft chest. Navy, charcoal or a muted earth tone. It should look like something you wear, not something you were issued. €250–600 buys something that survives a roadshow. Avoid: sheen, visible branding, patch pockets on anything formal, and anything with a contrasting collar.

Trousers

Plain, dark, flat-fronted, breaking once on the shoe or not at all. Wool or a wool blend for anything above the Nordic floor; a good chino below it. Denim only where the table above says so, and then only dark, undistressed and well-fitted.

Shoes

This is where founders lose the most for the least reason. One pair of clean, plain leather shoes — a derby, a loafer, or a minimal leather sneaker in the dressed-down markets. No square toes, no visible logos, no runners. Get them resoled before the roadshow rather than after it. A worn heel is visible from across a table and reads as disorder.

What to leave out

  • Ties, unless your buyer is a bank or a government.
  • Company-branded anything. Your own logo on your own chest at your own pitch reads as a costume.
  • Smart watches for anything photographed. They date the image within two years.
  • Backpacks in a partner meeting. A simple soft briefcase or nothing.

Fit, which is most of it

Fit outranks price by a wide margin. A €120 jacket that has been altered beats an €800 jacket that has not, and the difference is legible to people who could not tell you why. The four alterations that carry almost all of the return:

  1. Jacket sleeve length. End at the wrist bone, showing roughly a centimetre of shirt cuff. The most common fault anywhere, and around €20 to correct.
  2. Shirt body. Most off-the-peg shirts carry far too much fabric at the waist. Taken in through the side seams, about €25, and it changes the silhouette under a jacket entirely.
  3. Trouser hem and taper. One break or none, and the leg opening narrowed to suit the shoe. €15–30.
  4. Jacket shoulder. The exception — expensive to alter and usually not worth it. If the shoulder seam does not sit on the edge of your shoulder, the jacket is the wrong jacket. Check this first, in the shop, before anything else.

Shirt collars deserve their own note. A collar with no structure left in it collapses under a jacket and undoes everything above it. Collars die long before the shirt does. Replace the shirt, or wear the knit instead.

The roadshow problem

A raise is not one meeting, it is twenty-five to forty over a few compressed weeks, often across three or four cities, frequently with one carry-on. This changes the answer.

Build two outfits and rotate them. Nobody in your eleventh meeting attended your third. The audience for variety does not exist, and every additional garment is an untested variable — the shirt that creases in the bag, the shoes that turn out to hurt at hour six, the jacket that is wrong for a warm room.

Test the full rotation for a working day before you leave. Wear it for eight hours, sit in it, carry a bag in it, and look at a photograph of yourself in it at the end. Most failures are discovered this way for free, and discovered otherwise at the worst possible moment.

Pack for the warmest room, not the coldest street. Meeting rooms in February run hot, you will be adrenalised, and the layer you cannot remove is the one that betrays you. That is also the argument for the knit over the shirt-and-jacket: it survives a hot room, it survives a video call, and it does not crease in a bag.

A hypothetical seed founder doing London and Berlin in one week: charcoal merino crew, navy unstructured jacket, dark grey wool trousers, plain black derbies, worn as jacket-on in London and jacket-off in Berlin. One rotation, two cities, two formality floors, one bag.

Questions

Should I wear a suit to pitch investors?

In European venture, usually not. A full business suit reads as finance or enterprise sales rather than as a founder, and in Berlin, Amsterdam or Stockholm it can read as a misunderstanding of the room. The exceptions are real: fintech and insurance rooms where your customer is a bank, late-stage institutional meetings, and most rooms in Milan, Paris and Zurich, where the formality floor is genuinely higher.

What do I wear for a whole week of investor meetings?

Build two outfits, not seven. A dark knit and a mid-grey knit, one unstructured jacket, two pairs of trousers, one pair of shoes, worn in rotation. Nobody in meeting eleven saw meeting three. Variety is a cost you are paying for an audience that does not exist, and it introduces risk with every untested garment.

Does any of this matter more than the business?

No, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling. A strong company with a badly dressed founder raises money. The effect here is marginal — it shifts the temperature of a room rather than the outcome of a good meeting. It matters because at the margin, in a compressed process where you are being compared with eleven other founders that month, small consistent frictions add up and you never get told about them.

What is the single most common fault?

Sleeve length, by a wide margin. A jacket sleeve should end where the wrist meets the hand, showing roughly a centimetre of shirt cuff. Most founders' jackets are one to two centimetres too long, which swallows the hand and makes the whole garment read as borrowed. It costs about €20 to fix and it is the highest-return alteration available.

How much should I expect to spend?

Most founders need to spend less than they assume. A typical first pass is alterations on what you already own — sleeves, trouser hem, a shirt taken in through the body — for under €150 total, plus resoling or replacing one pair of shoes. Where new pieces are genuinely needed, a good unstructured jacket sits around €250–600 and a merino crew around €80–150.

Do investors actually notice clothes?

They do not consciously audit them, and most would sincerely deny it matters. That is exactly why it works the way it does. The judgement is fast, pre-verbal and attributed afterwards to something else — the founder seemed nervous, seemed junior, seemed not quite ready. Coherent appearance does not make you seem impressive; it removes a reason to discount you.
