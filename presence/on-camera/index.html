How should a founder look on camera, on stage, and in a pitch deck photo?

One face, three lighting conditions. On camera, raise the lens to eye level and face a window. On stage, go a tone darker and keep a defined shoulder line. In a deck photo, shoot at 85mm or longer.

These are three different physical problems that people treat as one aesthetic problem, which is why the usual advice fails. What follows is the mechanism in each case, and what to change.

On camera

A video call is the most-seen and least-considered image most founders have. You will be on camera more hours this quarter than you will be on stage in your life, and almost nobody has spent twenty minutes setting it up.

Lens height

The single largest variable. A laptop on a desk puts the camera around chin height, pointing up. That angle emphasises the underside of the jaw and the nostrils, shortens the forehead, and makes everyone look subtly worse. Raise the laptop until the lens sits at or just above eye level — a stack of books is sufficient and costs nothing. Use an external keyboard so you are not typing at shoulder height.

Distance

Laptop webcams are very wide lenses. At arm's length, that width visibly enlarges whatever is closest to the lens and compresses everything else. Sit back. Sixty to eighty centimetres rather than forty. You will occupy less of the frame, which is correct — a head that fills the frame reads as aggressive and shows the distortion at its worst. Aim for head and upper chest, with a little space above the head.

Light direction

Light should come from in front of you and slightly above. In practice this means facing a window, not sitting with one behind you. A window behind puts the camera's exposure in an impossible position and turns you into a silhouette. An overhead ceiling light alone drops shadow directly into the eye sockets, which reads as fatigue no matter how you feel.

If there is no usable window, any diffuse light source in front at roughly face height will do. It does not need to be a purpose-made lamp, and a single soft source beats an expensive ring light used badly.

Background

Depth beats decoration. A background with two or three metres behind you reads as a real space; a wall thirty centimetres behind your head reads as a cupboard. Avoid virtual backgrounds for anything that matters — the edge detection fails around hair and shoulders, and that failure is read as unseriousness. A plain wall with depth is better than a bookshelf with none.

Fabric on video

Video compression and sensor pattern make certain fabrics misbehave in ways they never do in person. Fine stripes, small checks and tight herringbone alias against the pixel grid and produce shimmer, and compression spends its bandwidth on that shimmer rather than on your face. Solid mid-tones are the reliable answer — navy, charcoal, slate, olive, deep burgundy.

On stage

Stage lighting is a different instrument: bright, hot, overwhelmingly from above, and usually cooler in colour temperature than you expect. Three consequences follow, and they are predictable enough to dress for.

Colour washes out. A mid-grey reads as light grey; a soft blue reads as near-white. Go one step darker and one step more saturated than you would for a meeting. This is the only context where the advice runs in that direction.

Shadows deepen. Top-down light means everything horizontal catches light and everything vertical falls into shadow, including your eye sockets. You cannot fix this from the stage, but you can avoid making it worse: a heavy fringe or a deep brow shadow becomes a mask under stage light.

Shape flattens. Texture disappears and the silhouette does all the work. This is the one situation where a jacket earns its place over a knit, because a defined shoulder line survives the flattening and a soft crew neck does not. It is also why pure white is a mistake — it clips to a flat blank patch with no detail, and pure black collapses into a shape with no internal definition at all.

Two practical notes. Check whether you are being given a lapel microphone, which needs a lapel or a firm placket to clip to and is genuinely awkward on a crew neck; ask the organiser in advance. And if there is a step up to the stage, your shoes are photographed from below by every person in the first three rows.

The pitch deck photograph

The team slide is looked at for a few seconds and remembered longer than you would like. It is also the image most likely to be years out of date, because nobody schedules replacing it.

The brief to hand a photographer

Most founders do not know what to ask for and get whatever the photographer defaults to. This is the whole brief, and it is short enough to send as a message:

  • 85mm to 135mm on full frame. Longer lenses compress facial features favourably and keep the photographer at a natural distance. Anything wider than 50mm enlarges the nose.
  • Soft light from slightly above and to one side, with fill on the shadow side. Not flat-on, which erases structure; not hard, which carves it.
  • A mid-tone background with separation — not white, not black, and not pressed against a wall. Grey seamless is the safe default and reads as neutral rather than corporate.
  • Eyes level with the lens. Not looking up, not looking down.
  • Frame at chest or waist. Enough to show posture and shoulder line; not a passport crop.

Posture and hands

No crossed arms. It is read as closed and defensive by a large share of viewers, it is the single most common pose in bad founder photography, and it makes the upper body a solid block. Hands either out of frame or relaxed. Shoulders back and down, weight slightly on the back foot, chin very slightly forward and down — the last one feels wrong and photographs correctly, which is why professionals do it and nobody else does.

Recency beats flattery

A photograph that no longer matches you is worse than a plainer photograph that does. If an investor sees the deck on Monday and you in person on Thursday, any gap between the two has to be reconciled, and that reconciliation is a small cost paid at exactly the wrong moment. Replace every two years, and immediately after any change in hair, weight or facial hair.

The team slide as a set

Five founders photographed by five different photographers in five different years, at five different crops and colour temperatures, is a slide that reads as a company that does not coordinate. One session, one photographer, one background, one day, is worth more than five individually better photographs. If a co-founder joins later, send the new photographer the original file and ask them to match it.

One face across three conditions

The point of treating these together is that a person researching you will encounter all three within a few minutes — the deck photo, a conference recording, and then you on a call. Each can be individually decent while the set is incoherent, and the set is what gets read.

Coherence does not mean identical. It means a person who sees all three does not have to do any work to conclude they are looking at the same person. Same approximate weight, same hair length within a reasonable range, same register of dress, same colour family. This is what the third area of the diagnostic measures, and it is the area where founders most often score lowest without ever having considered it.

Specimen — illustrative only, not a client

A hypothetical founder whose deck photo is a 2021 studio shot in a white shirt, whose conference recording is from a stage in a black t-shirt, and who takes calls in a hoodie against a bare wall. Every image is defensible. The set describes three different people, and the viewer resolves that inconsistency by discounting all three.

Questions

Why do I look worse on my webcam than in the mirror?

Three reasons, all physical rather than personal. The lens is usually below eye level, which is an unflattering angle on anyone. It is a very wide lens held close, which enlarges whatever is nearest it — normally your nose and forehead. And the light is typically overhead or from behind, which puts shadow in your eye sockets. Raise the laptop, sit back, and put a window in front of you, and most of it resolves.

What focal length should a founder headshot be shot at?

Between 85mm and 135mm on full frame. Below about 50mm the lens has to be close enough to visibly enlarge the nose and narrow the ears, which is the effect people read as an unflattering photo. Give your photographer that number directly — it is the single most useful instruction a non-photographer can give.

What should I not wear on camera?

Fine stripes, small checks and tight herringbone, which alias against the sensor and shimmer or strobe on video. Pure white, which clips to a flat blank under stage lighting. Pure black, which collapses into a silhouette with no shape. And anything with a visible logo, which dates the footage. Mid-tone solids in navy, charcoal, slate, olive or deep burgundy are safe in every condition.

How often should a founder headshot be replaced?

Every two years, and immediately after any significant change in hair, weight or facial hair. The test is not whether the photograph is good, it is whether someone who met you afterwards would think it was taken recently. A photograph that flatters but no longer matches you creates a small reconciliation cost the moment you walk in, which is worse than a plainer photograph that is accurate.

Does stage lighting change what I should wear?

Substantially. Stage light is almost always hot, bright and from above, which washes colour out by roughly a tone, deepens every shadow, and flattens texture. Go one step darker and one step more saturated than you would for a meeting, and prioritise a defined shoulder line, because the top-down light is destroying your natural vertical definition.

Do I need a professional photographer?

For the deck photo and anything on a website, yes — the difference between a competent portrait and a phone photograph is visible to everyone. For everything else, no. Most of the improvement available to a founder is in camera height, distance and light direction, none of which cost anything.
