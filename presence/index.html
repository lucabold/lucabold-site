What is presence advisory, and who is it for?

Presence advisory is remote, private work on how you are read before you speak: dress, grooming, framing and the consistency of your public image. It is for founders, investors and operators whose appearance now carries commercial weight.

It is sold as advisory because that is what it is. You are not buying clothes or a shopping companion. You are buying a trained second opinion on a channel you cannot see yourself using, applied to a specific set of rooms you are walking into in the next ninety days.

The credential

A decade as a working international model across eight markets — Cape Town, Milan, Shanghai, Hong Kong, Seoul, Bali, Mumbai and Istanbul. That work is not a qualification in taste. It is ten years of being professionally assessed on exactly this question, in about three seconds, thousands of times, by casting directors and clients whose money depended on reading a person correctly and quickly.

That is the whole basis of this service, and it is worth being precise about why it matters. Most advice about how founders should look is written by people who have never been on the receiving end of the judgement. They theorise about it. A casting room is the judgement, repeated all day, with immediate and unambiguous feedback: you are right for it or you are not, and you find out in seconds.

Ten years of that builds one specific skill — the ability to look at a person and say what they are currently being read as, and what small, cheap, unglamorous changes would move that reading. Not what is fashionable. Not what would look good on someone else. What you are transmitting right now, and what is costing you.

Competitors in this category sell certifications and courses. There is nothing wrong with that, but it is a different thing: a body of taught theory versus a decade of direct practice at the exact task. I am not certified in image consulting and do not claim to be.

What actually gets decided in three seconds

People routinely overestimate how much of a first impression is about attractiveness and underestimate how much is about coherence. The fast read is not “is this person good-looking”. It is closer to: does everything about this person agree with everything else, and does it agree with the situation?

Incoherence is what gets noticed, and it is nearly always specific and fixable:

  • A jacket that fits in the body but is a full size too long in the sleeve, so the hand disappears.
  • Expensive shoes with a worn heel and a dull toe, which reads as borrowed rather than owned.
  • A haircut at week nine of a six-week cut, which reads as distraction rather than deliberate.
  • A shirt collar with no structure left in it, collapsing under a jacket — the single most common fault I see on founders on stage.
  • A deck photo taken in a different decade, at a different weight, with different hair, three slides before you walk into the room in person.

None of these are expensive to fix. All of them are doing work against you in the first three seconds, and none of them will be mentioned to you by anyone, ever, because there is no polite way for an investor to raise it.

That silence is the actual problem. The channel is loud and nobody gives you feedback on it.

Who it is for

Founders with a raise in front of them

The clearest case. You are about to be in twenty-five to forty first meetings in a compressed window, most of them with people who will make a provisional judgement about you before you finish the first slide, and you will be photographed and filmed throughout. The cost of a bad read is not a rejection you can trace — it is a slightly cooler room that you never learn the reason for.

Operators stepping into a public-facing role

A technical founder becoming a CEO. A second-in-command taking the stage for the first time. Someone promoted into a role where the audience is now external rather than internal. The old defaults were calibrated for a room of people who already knew you were competent. The new room does not.

Investors raising a fund

The mirror image of the founder case, and structurally the harder one: you are asking institutions for money while being read for judgement and stability rather than energy and upside. The formality calibration is different and most people get it wrong in the same direction.

People with a recurring public schedule

Conference circuits, board seats, recorded interviews, a podcast run. Here the issue stops being any one appearance and becomes consistency — whether a person searching you finds one coherent individual or six different ones.

The four areas

Everything is assessed across four areas. The free diagnostic scores you on the same four, three questions each.

  1. In person. How you are read in a room: fit, condition, grooming interval, formality calibration, and whether the whole thing agrees with itself.
  2. On camera. Video calls, stage, and photography. A different problem with different physics — lens height, focal length, lighting direction and fabric behaviour all change the read independently of what you own.
  3. Consistency across your public images. Every photograph of you that a person can find, considered as one set rather than individually.
  4. Situational fit for your next ninety days. The only area that is time-bound, and the one that decides priority. An excellent general wardrobe that is wrong for the six situations actually coming up is not an asset.

Questions

Is this styling?

No. A stylist dresses you for an occasion and leaves. Presence advisory works on the system underneath: what you own, what you are photographed in, how those images agree with each other, and how all of it is calibrated to the specific rooms you are walking into over the next quarter. The output is judgement and a plan, not a shopping bag.

Do I have to be in Europe?

No. The work is remote and the advisory is the same wherever you are. What changes is the calibration — a room in Stockholm, Munich, Milan and London each read formality differently, and the plan accounts for the ones you are actually going to be in.

What is the credential here?

A decade working as an international model across eight markets — Cape Town, Milan, Shanghai, Hong Kong, Seoul, Bali, Mumbai and Istanbul. That work consists of being assessed on exactly this, in about three seconds, by people whose job is to make that judgement fast and commercially. This is a practitioner credential, not a certification.

Will you tell me to buy expensive clothes?

Usually the opposite. Most founders own more than they need and wear the wrong third of it. The first pass is almost always subtraction, repair and alteration. Where something does need buying, it is named specifically, with a price band, and you buy it yourself.

Can I get something useful without hiring you?

Yes, and that is deliberate. The free twelve-question diagnostic returns a scored result and three specific fixes with no email required to read it. These pages are also written to be usable on their own — if you never enquire, you should still leave with something you can act on this week.

What do you not do?

No confidence coaching, no public-speaking training, no personal branding in the social-media sense, no ghostwriting, and no in-person shopping trips. If your problem is what you say rather than how you are read before you say it, this is the wrong service and I will tell you so.
