How is a non-European founder read when raising in Europe?

Mostly on formality calibration rather than origin. The common misread is over-formality: a full suit in a room of knitwear signals unfamiliarity with the market, which investors tend to hear as distance from their customers.

This page is about legibility, not assimilation. Nothing here suggests looking less like yourself. The variable actually being read is narrower and far more practical than that, and it can be adjusted in an afternoon.

The variable that is actually being read

Founders raising outside their home market often assume they are being read on a broad, uncomfortable axis of belonging. In the specific context of a venture meeting, that is mostly not what is happening. The fast judgement is narrower and more mundane: does this person know what kind of room this is?

Formality is the most visible proxy for that knowledge, because formality conventions are strictly local, unwritten, and learned by being in a place. Getting them wrong is one of the few things in a first meeting that genuinely signals distance from a market — and market knowledge is a thing investors are explicitly evaluating.

This is why the correction is not about identity. It is about learning one local convention, in the same way you would learn how a term sheet is customarily structured in that market. Everything else that makes you visibly from somewhere else is not the problem and does not need solving.

Where this read comes from: a decade working as an international model across eight markets — Cape Town, Milan, Shanghai, Hong Kong, Seoul, Bali, Mumbai and Istanbul. Working across that many markets is, among other things, a decade of arriving somewhere and having to calibrate to a local standard quickly, and being assessed on the result in about three seconds.

The over-correction, in both directions

Over-formality

The more common of the two, and it comes from applying a good rule in the wrong place. In a great many business cultures, a suit is the correct and respectful default for an important meeting with someone senior. Founders from those markets are not making an error of judgement; they are making an error of translation.

In most European venture rooms the suit reads as finance, enterprise sales or consulting, not as a founder. Worse, a visibly new suit reads as effort made for the meeting, which the convention codes as inexperience. The founder has signalled respect and the room has received something closer to unfamiliarity.

Over-correction downward

The less common failure but a more damaging one. A founder who has been told European venture is casual arrives in a hoodie and sneakers that are not theirs in any real sense, and the discomfort is legible. Clothing that a person is not at home in always reads as costume, and that is a worse signal than being slightly overdressed.

There is also a floor. Berlin being casual does not mean condition stops being read — the Nordic and German rooms are relaxed about formality and genuinely attentive to quality and upkeep. Casual is not the absence of a standard; it is a different standard.

The working rule: move one step, not three. If your default for an important meeting is a suit and tie, the correct adjustment for a Berlin seed room is the same suit trousers with a fine knit and no tie — not a wardrobe you have never worn. Recognisably yourself, calibrated.

Calibrating before you land

The formality floor across European venture markets is set out in detail on what a founder should wear to pitch investors in Europe. In summary: Berlin and the Nordics sit lowest, Amsterdam close behind, London has the broadest band, Munich and Zurich run meaningfully higher, and Paris and Milan sit highest.

But the general table is a starting point, not the answer. The specific firm beats the general market every time, and the research takes ten minutes:

  1. Partner photographs on the firm's own site. The most reliable single signal available. It is how they have chosen to present themselves.
  2. Recent conference footage of the partner you are meeting. Discount one notch, since stage runs above meetings.
  3. Team slides of two or three portfolio companies. This shows you what the founders they have already backed look like.
  4. The office. A firm on a converted industrial floor and a firm in a private-bank building are not the same room.

Do this once per firm on the list. It converts an anxious open question into a specific, answerable one.

What travel does to the read

A founder flying in for a raise is carrying a disadvantage that has nothing to do with origin and gets mistaken for something else. Fatigue is read quickly and interpreted as a character trait rather than a circumstance — low energy, hesitancy, lack of conviction. Nobody adjusts for the fact that you landed at 06:00.

  • Arrive two nights early if the round matters. One night is not enough to clear a long flight and the second night is the one that does the work.
  • Pack for creasing. Fine merino comes out of a bag ready; cotton shirts do not. Hang everything on arrival, and use the bathroom steam if there is no time for anything else.
  • Do not debut anything. A roadshow is the worst possible place to discover that shoes hurt at hour six or that a collar sits wrong. Everything in the bag should have been worn for a full working day already.
  • Check the weather properly. Northern Europe between November and March is darker, colder and wetter than most people plan for, and a soaked founder in the lobby starts the meeting behind.
  • Fix the camera before the trip. A meaningful share of a cross-border process happens on video, and the first read of you is often on a screen. That setup is covered on the on-camera page.

What not to change

It is worth being explicit, because advice in this category often slides into telling people to erase themselves.

Cultural and religious dress is not a liability and does not need managing. What gets read on any garment, anywhere, is the same three things: whether it fits, whether it has been looked after, and whether it agrees with everything else you have on. A well-fitted, well-maintained garment reads as deliberate in every market I have worked in. A poorly fitted or neglected one reads as careless in all of them too. The category is not what is being assessed; the execution is.

Accent, name and background are likewise not presence problems and I have nothing useful to say about them, because there is nothing to fix. The scope of this work is narrow on purpose: fit, condition, grooming interval, formality calibration, and the coherence of your public images. That is a real and limited set of variables, all of which are within your control and none of which require you to be different.

Questions

Should I dress more European to raise in Europe?

No. You should dress legibly for the specific room, which is a narrower and more practical question. Attempting to look European is both unnecessary and ineffective — it reads as imitation, which is a worse signal than being visibly from somewhere else. What actually needs adjusting is formality level, which is a local convention anyone can learn in an afternoon, not identity.

Is a full business suit a mistake?

In most European venture rooms outside Paris, Milan and Zurich, yes — but it is a mistake of calibration, not of taste. In many markets a suit is the correct and respectful default for an important meeting, so founders arriving from those markets are applying a sound rule to a room that runs on a different one. The fix is to learn the local floor, not to abandon a sense of occasion.

Does keeping cultural or religious dress hurt a raise?

It should not, and in practice the things that get read are condition, fit and coherence rather than category. A well-fitted, well-maintained garment of any kind reads as deliberate. The failure mode is not wearing it — it is wearing something that does not fit properly or has not been looked after, which is read the same way anywhere in the world.

How do I find out what a specific firm's rooms are like?

Look at their own material. Partner photographs on the firm's site, recent conference footage, portfolio-company team slides. Ten minutes of that tells you more than any general guide, including this one. If the partners are photographed in open-necked shirts with no jackets, that is the room you are walking into.

I am flying in the day before. What actually matters?

Sleep and the condition of what you are wearing, in that order. Visible fatigue is read as low energy and that judgement is made before you speak. Arrive two nights early if the round matters. Hang everything the moment you land, and have one garment that survives a bag without creasing — a fine merino knit does, a cotton shirt does not.

Is any of this fair?

No. A quick read on appearance is a crude instrument and it misjudges people constantly. The reason to address it is not that it is fair but that it is operating whether or not you engage with it, and the adjustments involved are small, cheap and reversible. Nothing here asks you to be someone else.
